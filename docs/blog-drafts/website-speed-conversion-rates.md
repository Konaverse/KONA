# How Website Speed Affects Conversion Rates and What to Measure

*By Konstantinos Kyprianou*

Website speed can affect conversion rates by changing how quickly visitors see your offer and complete an enquiry, booking or purchase. Published experiments show that performance improvements can increase sales or help visitors advance through a website. The size of that improvement depends on the business and the change. A 2.5% conversion rate needs the same context: which action, which visitors and what value it produces.

Suppose your latest campaign is bringing the right people to a service page. You have spent time explaining the offer, selecting relevant work and making the next step clear.

Someone decides to book a call. They tap the button, wait for the booking tool and wonder whether the tap registered. They try again.

At that point, your website is asking for patience from someone who has already shown interest. That is a useful place to investigate performance because the delay sits directly between intention and action.

I want the effort you put into earning attention to carry through to a completed task. If you paid to bring that person to the page, a delay at the booking step deserves the same attention as the campaign that brought them there.

## How speed can affect the conversion journey

Loading, responsiveness and stability influence different moments in a visit. Someone needs to see the service explanation, use the navigation and complete the form or booking process.

If the explanation appears late, they may leave before evaluating it. If a button responds slowly, they may be uncertain whether it worked. If content shifts while they are tapping, they may select something they did not intend to select.

Those problems give you specific behaviors to investigate. A headline number for the whole website will not tell you where a task becomes difficult.

For example, faster loading on a service page may help more people reach the enquiry form. But if the form is confusing or its submission fails, the final outcome can remain disappointing. Review the whole journey, including the services connected to it.

[The three-second website rule article](/blog/three-second-rule-website-design) explains the performance metrics and first-visit checks. Here, the next step is to connect the experience to an action the business values.

## What published experiments actually show

There is useful evidence for investing in performance. The details of the experiment matter as much as the headline result.

### Vodafone: faster loading and more sales

In a [case study published in March 2021](https://web.dev/case-studies/vodafone), Vodafone reported an A/B test of a landing page. Half the traffic went to a performance-optimized version and half to the baseline. The versions were visually and functionally identical.

The optimized version had a 31% improvement in real-user Largest Contentful Paint, or LCP, and generated 8% more sales. LCP measures when the largest eligible image or text block in the visible area is rendered.

The controlled comparison supports the business case for that performance treatment. Use it as a reason to investigate the experience on your own website; measure the resulting lift against your own baseline.

### Trendyol: responsiveness and the next step

A [Trendyol case study updated in December 2023](https://web.dev/case-studies/trendyol-inp) describes an A/B test following improvements to its product listing page. It reported a 50% reduction in Interaction to Next Paint, or INP, alongside a 1% uplift in click-through from listings to product details per user session.

That result concerns progress to a product page. It is not a reported 1% increase in purchases. INP measures responsiveness to interactions, so this example also extends the performance discussion beyond initial loading.

For your business, the comparable question might be whether improving a slow interaction helps more people reach the booking step. You would then check completed bookings separately.

## How much does a one-second improvement increase conversions?

There is no universal multiplier. Saving a second where it prevents a visitor from completing a task may be valuable. Saving a second from work that does not affect that task may have a different result.

The familiar smaller-delay statistics need context too. Deloitte's [2020 Milliseconds Make Millions study](https://www.deloitte.com/ie/en/services/consulting/research/milliseconds-make-millions.html) observed mobile performance and business metrics over four weeks. It reported an 8.4% increase in retail conversions associated with a natural 0.1-second speed improvement. That is a study finding from those conditions, not a formula you can multiply by ten to predict the value of a second.

My starting point is the part of your visitor's experience that changes. A useful performance proposal identifies the bottleneck, explains the task it affects and states how the improvement will be assessed.

## Is a 2.5% conversion rate good?

A 2.5% conversion rate can be commercially valuable, but the number alone cannot tell you whether the website is performing well.

Start by defining the action and denominator. For a session-based enquiry rate, use:

**Sessions with at least one completed enquiry ÷ sessions included in the analysis × 100.**

If 25 out of 1,000 sessions include a completed enquiry, the rate is 2.5%. The outcome is different if those 25 sessions contain only clicks on the contact button.

[Google Analytics defines session key event rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/table/13948007?hl=en) as the share of sessions in which a key event happened. A key event represents an action marked as important to the business. Check which event the report includes before treating it as an enquiry or a sale.

Then ask who is represented in those sessions. All website traffic, visitors to a specific service page and people arriving from a targeted campaign are different groups. A broad educational article may attract useful readers who are not ready to enquire during that visit.

Finally, examine quality and economics. Twenty-five suitable enquiries can be valuable to a specialist business. Twenty-five irrelevant submissions create work without the same opportunity.

Judge the rate against a consistent baseline, comparable traffic and the resulting business value. Those give you a sound basis for deciding what needs attention.

## Follow the improvement through to business value

Here is an illustrative comparison, using the same session-based enquiry definition in both cases. These figures are assumptions for explaining the calculation.

| Measure | Baseline example | Improved-rate example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sessions included | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Sessions with a completed enquiry | 250 | 300 |
| Enquiry conversion rate | 2.5% | 3.0% |

The difference is 50 additional enquiry sessions. The rate has increased by **0.5 percentage points**, which is a **20% relative increase** from 2.5%. Those are two ways of describing the same change.

The table does not establish that speed caused the increase. That requires evidence about how the comparison was made.

To explore the possible commercial value, suppose each additional enquiry session produces one distinct, genuine enquiry. If 40% qualify and 25% of those qualified enquiries become projects, the 50 additional enquiries represent 20 qualified opportunities and an estimated five projects. Those qualification and win rates are assumptions too; substitute your own observed figures.

You can then estimate the added contribution using:

**Additional qualified enquiries × win rate × average contribution per won project.**

Contribution here means revenue remaining after direct delivery costs. It is different from gross revenue and total company profit. Compare the estimated added contribution with implementation and ongoing costs over the same period.

Also check whether the team has capacity to deliver the additional work and whether the enquiries actually reach that outcome. For services with a long sales process, won projects need time to emerge.

This is why I prefer a discussion that follows enquiries through qualification and sales. It connects the website to the business without assuming every submission has the value of a completed project.

## How to measure whether speed made the difference

Agree on the measurement before making the change. That makes the result easier to interpret and prevents the team from choosing whichever metric improved afterwards.

### 1. Verify the completed action

Submit a genuine test enquiry and confirm that the receiving system records it. Check that the measurement distinguishes a completed action from a button click or an attempted submission.

Keep repeated event triggers separate from the number of converting sessions. [Google Analytics allows different key-event counting methods](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13366706?hl=en), so raw event counts and session rates can describe different things. Document the configuration and avoid changing it halfway through a comparison.

### 2. Choose a relevant part of the journey

Start with a page or interaction that has a measurable problem and a meaningful role in conversion. That might be a service landing page, a product listing or a booking step.

Record the audience and traffic included. Separate mobile from desktop and review relevant traffic sources. The performance measurements and business outcomes should describe a comparable population.

### 3. Compare versions while controlling other changes

Where traffic and tooling allow, use a concurrent A/B test with consistent visitor assignment. Keep the offer, copy, design and functionality the same across the baseline and improved versions.

[Google's guidance on speed and business metrics](https://web.dev/articles/site-speed-and-business-metrics) describes this approach. Before testing, agree on the main outcome, the effect worth detecting and the data needed to assess it.

The test setup needs review too. Some experimentation tools can delay displaying the page, as [Google's guidance for business decision makers explains](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cwv-business). Verify the experience each group actually receives.

### 4. Report what the result can support

Report the performance change, conversion outcome, sample size and uncertainty together. Include lead quality or completed sales when the reporting period allows.

For a before-and-after comparison, record changes in campaigns, traffic mix, offers and seasonality. These can also affect conversions, so the comparison alone cannot isolate speed's contribution.

Few enquiries can mean a long wait for a conclusive experiment. You can still fix a demonstrably slow or broken interaction. Record the improvement and treat later conversion trends as observations with that uncertainty.

## Improve the experience that helps people act

A premium website can include distinctive imagery and motion while giving visitors a clear, responsive route to the next step. The priorities should follow what those visitors need to do.

If opening the booking tool is slow, investigate that step. If the main content appears late, investigate its delivery. If people submit the form but enquiries disappear, resolve that failure before debating small changes to loading time.

For your next review, bring together the performance evidence, completed-action rate and quality of the resulting enquiries. Ask the team to propose a change whose effect can be verified.

That is the business case I want from a [custom web development project](/services/web-development): an experience that supports the action you need, with evidence that helps you decide where to invest next.
