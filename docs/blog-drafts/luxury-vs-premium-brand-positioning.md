# Luxury vs. Premium: Choosing the Right Position for Your Brand

*By Konstantinos Kyprianou*

Premium positioning emphasizes the quality, performance or service that makes an offer worth a higher price than relevant alternatives. Luxury positioning places greater weight on the desirability and meaning of the particular brand: its identity, prestige and connection with customers. The right choice is the position your audience values and your business can deliver consistently.

If you have put years of care into a business, you want its website to reflect that effort. You want someone to notice the details, understand the standard of your work and feel comfortable choosing you.

That ambition deserves a specific brief.

When a website brief uses the word luxury, I want to understand what the customer is being invited to value. It could be a reliably better result, a particular creative vision or a service shaped around their individual preferences. Those answers affect the words, evidence and experience we need to build.

A position becomes useful when it helps you make those decisions.

## What is the difference between premium and luxury?

Premium gives a customer reasons to value an offer more highly within a category. Depending on your business, that could mean better materials, specialist expertise, a more dependable process or stronger support after purchase.

Luxury gives greater weight to the desire for this particular brand and the experience associated with it. Craftsmanship, a recognizable creative identity, personal meaning and prestige can contribute to that desire.

There is considerable overlap. A premium brand can create an emotional connection. A luxury brand still needs convincing quality and useful service. For a website brief, I would use the distinction to decide what deserves emphasis:

| Decision | Premium emphasis | Luxury emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain the value | Show the standard of the offer and why it matters to the buyer. | Show the distinctive world, work and experience associated with the brand. |
| Present evidence | Make quality, expertise, process and support easy to assess. | Make creative authorship, craft, meaningful distinctions and service tangible. |
| Develop the relationship | Help the customer choose confidently and rely on delivery. | Help the customer connect with the brand and experience its particular character. |

These are overlapping emphases for planning the content. The table is a working lens, rather than a formal classification test.

## What qualifies a brand as luxury?

The customer's perception matters. A [2019 literature review by Ko, Costello and Taylor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296317302850) proposed a definition combining perceived quality, authentic value, prestige, a price customers consider justified and a deep connection with the brand. It also acknowledged the lack of a widely accepted definition.

That gives you a useful question to investigate: what do the people you want to serve recognize and value about your business?

A founder's description of the brand is one input. Customer accounts, the work itself, relevant recognition and the experience of buying from you give the description substance.

An established history can be meaningful when it explains a practice or tradition that continues in the work. A newer business can present its actual origins, the founder's training and the creative decisions that make it recognizable. Explain the history you have, then build recognition through the work you deliver.

Availability needs the same care. If individual commissions take time, explain the process and realistic lead times. If you accept a limited number of projects, make that information accurate and help a prospective customer understand how to enquire.

The website should make those qualities visible and understandable. Its design can support the position, while the business supplies the reasons to believe it.

## Two examples of how brands explain their position

[Miele's company philosophy](https://www.miele.com/en/com/philosophy-2095.htm) explicitly places its domestic-appliance brand in the premium segment. It connects that position with long product life, performance, comfortable everyday use, energy efficiency, design and customer support. It also describes a family business founded in 1899.

Premium, in that account, carries ambition and a long history. The reasons the company gives customers concern what the products and business should deliver.

[Hermès describes its strategy](https://finance.hermes.com/en/strategy/) around creation, craftsmanship and a carefully managed distribution network. Its account connects the objects to the house's creative identity, artisanal knowledge and continuity over generations. Its [after-sales service](https://finance.hermes.com/en/circular-economy/) also provides care, repair and restoration for existing objects.

The overlap is useful: both businesses discuss quality and lasting use. Hermès gives creative identity and the house's craft tradition a central role in explaining its particular world.

My takeaway for your website is to make the explanation specific to your business. A visitor should encounter the product, expertise or experience that supports the position, with enough detail to understand it.

## Which is better for your business: luxury or premium?

The better position fits the purchase your customer is making and the business you can sustain. Treat it as a choice about your offer, audience and operations.

If customers choose you for specialist knowledge, a dependable outcome and attentive support, a premium position may explain your value well. You can make those advantages concrete and give the buyer a clear basis for comparison.

If customers seek your particular creative world, the meaning of owning your work or a distinctive personal experience, luxury positioning may be appropriate to investigate. It needs evidence that those qualities matter to the intended audience.

The same person can make different choices across purchases. Someone might buy a premium appliance for dependable daily use and choose a luxury object for its personal significance. Income alone gives you an incomplete picture of what they want from either purchase.

The [2026 luxury-client report from The Business of Fashion and McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/de/~/media/mckinsey/locations/europe%20and%20middle%20east/deutschland/news/presse/2026/2026-07-06%20state%20of%20luxury/the-state-of-fashion-face-to-face-with-luxury-clients.pdf) found emotional connection ranked first among drivers of brand desirability in its US and China survey. It also reported scepticism toward artificial scarcity. Those findings concern the clients and markets studied; use them as a reason to investigate your own customers' priorities.

I would begin with conversations about why people chose you, what gave them confidence and what they appreciated after delivery. Discuss alternatives they considered and the occasion or problem that made the purchase relevant. Look for patterns you can support with further evidence.

## Four questions to answer before choosing your position

For a positioning discussion, I want the team to be able to answer these questions in ordinary language.

### 1. What are customers paying you to do well?

Describe the value as the customer experiences it. For a specialist service, that might be reliable guidance through a difficult decision. For a maker, it could be an object that fits a particular space and improves daily use.

Then identify where the brand itself contributes to the choice: a recognizable design approach, a relationship with the creator or the meaning of the work to the buyer. Ask customers about those possibilities and compare their answers with your assumptions.

This helps you decide which parts of the offer deserve the strongest emphasis.

### 2. What can you show to support the promise?

Connect each important claim to something a customer can assess.

If you emphasize specialist expertise, show who performs the work and explain relevant decisions in a case study. If you emphasize craft, show the process and finished details with useful context. If personal service matters, describe what it involves from the first conversation through delivery and aftercare.

The evidence needs to match your role. A beautiful finished project becomes more informative when the reader understands what you contributed and how the work was carried out.

### 3. What does the position require from the business?

Look at the work behind the promise. Individual attention requires people with time to provide it. A distinctive product may depend on particular materials, production methods or creative direction. Strong aftercare needs a service the customer can actually reach.

Review pricing, capacity, lead times and delivery responsibilities together. The business needs a workable way to provide the experience being promised.

I want these decisions in the brief because they affect the enquiry process. A standard purchase, an individual commission and a complex consultancy engagement can need different information and next steps.

### 4. Does the position hold together across your offer?

Review your services or collections as a whole. Identify which promise they share and where the scope differs.

You may have a standard service and a more involved bespoke engagement. Explain who each suits, what each includes and how the experience changes. A carefully designed range can let people enter the relationship at different levels without leaving them uncertain about the standard of the business.

Give someone a clear account of what they will receive whichever route fits their needs.

## How the position should change your website

Start with the message and evidence. Agree on the visual expression once the team understands what it needs to communicate.

For a premium position, make the offer and its advantages easy to evaluate. Show relevant work, explain the process, introduce the people responsible and give buyers useful information about scope and support. The visitor needs to understand what makes choosing you worthwhile.

For a luxury position, give the brand's particular character enough depth to be appreciated. Show the creative authorship, origins, materials and experiences that matter to the customer. Let the work establish a recognizable point of view, with clear routes to explore it and begin a relationship.

In both cases, provide the practical information a visitor needs. If a price depends on scope, explain how an enquiry becomes a proposal. If appointments are part of the service, tell people how to arrange one and what to expect. Choose the process according to the actual offer.

I want the promises in the copy to appear in the experience. If personal attention is central to the position, the contact process should explain who responds and what happens next. Keep that explanation aligned with what the team can provide.

[The article on making a website look luxury](/blog/how-to-make-a-website-look-luxury) covers typography, imagery, spacing and motion. This positioning work gives those choices a reason to belong to your brand.

## A positioning example for a specialist maker

Consider two hypothetical furniture workshops with different offers.

The first makes fitted cabinetry for homeowners who value precise installation, durable materials and dependable project management. A premium brief could lead with how the work fits the home, explain the design and installation process, and show completed projects with scope and material details. The enquiry should help the customer understand the next step toward a suitable proposal.

The second develops individually commissioned pieces around a recognizable designer's work. Buyers may value its creative authorship, the relationship involved in commissioning and the significance of the finished piece. A luxury-positioning brief could give the design approach and making process more depth, show previous work clearly, and explain how a commission develops with the client.

These are different business propositions with different reasons to choose them. The second still needs convincing information about materials, practical use, lead times and aftercare. Its intended luxury position also needs to be tested against customer perception.

That is the level of specificity I want in a design brief. The team can develop an experience around a real offer and assess whether the words, evidence and interactions support it.

## Write the promise your business can stand behind

Bring three things to your next website discussion: a description of the intended customer, a precise account of what they value, and the evidence your business can show.

Use those to write a short positioning brief:

**We serve [customer and occasion]. They choose us for [specific value]. We demonstrate that through [credible evidence], and deliver the experience through [actual process and service].**

Complete it in words your team and customers would recognize. Then review the website against that promise.

For a [web design project](/services/web-design), I want this clarity early. It gives the creative and technical work a shared purpose: helping people understand the business you have built and take a next step that makes sense for them.
